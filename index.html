Who carries the city's earthquake risk, and who gets to see the data?

Field note 01

The problem

At 00:09 on 23 January 2024, Almaty woke up shaking. An earthquake of magnitude 6.7, with its epicentre about 264 kilometres south-east of the city near the Kyrgyzstan–China border, reached 5 points in Almaty on the MSK-64 intensity scale (Daribay, 2024). No warning came from the 112 rescue service. People ran out into the snow in slippers and without coats, roads out of the city jammed, and queues formed at petrol stations. Some of the official gathering points for residents, mostly schools, turned out to be closed. Nobody died and no buildings collapsed, but 44 people sought medical help for injuries (Rakhimzhan, 2024).

That night was a warning, not a disaster. Almaty sits in one of the most seismically active areas of Central Asia. The city's seismic microzoning map places it in a 9–10 point zone, it is crossed by 27 tectonic faults, and up to 200 weak earthquakes occur within 80 kilometres of it every year (Kazinform, 2024). The region's history shows what a strong quake can do: the 1911 earthquake killed more than 450 people in the area (GFDRR, 2015).

The city knows how exposed it is. Seismic audits in 2017–2018 and 2023–2024 covered 21,441 buildings, and 26% of them, about 5,600, need seismic strengthening or demolition (Kazinform, 2024). In the first audit alone, more than 3,000 buildings were found not to be earthquake-resistant, and 2,339 of them were apartment buildings, in a city with more than 9,600 apartment buildings in total (BES.media, 2026).

The effort is growing. Based on the 2023–2024 audits, the city approved an action plan for 2026–2035 to improve the earthquake resistance of residential and social buildings (Akimat of Almaty, 2026). Under its 2026–2030 development plan, it also intends to screen about 200,000 buildings, give each one a "seismic passport", and collect the results in a single digital register (Milayarova, 2026).

Knowing the risk has not meant fixing it for residents. The city's emergency department says strengthening work continues, but schools, hospitals and other public buildings come first, partly because they serve as official shelters in an emergency (BES.media, 2026). The 2026–2030 plan follows the same order: it aims to strengthen at least 150 key life-support facilities, about 400 social facilities, and at least 200 apartment buildings (Milayarova, 2026). Two hundred is fewer than one in ten of the 2,339 apartment buildings flagged in the first audit, although some may have been repaired or demolished since. The wider renovation programme for dilapidated housing shows the same gap between plans and buildings: 670 owners moved into new flats in 2025, meeting the resettlement target, but only 18 old houses were demolished, against 78 planned (Vladimirskaya, 2026; 24.kz, 2025).

Residents also cannot easily see the information these decisions rest on. In 2019, officials promised to publish every building's passport online (Informburo, 2019). Instead, the head of the city's urban planning department said in 2025 that the new database would have only limited access: emergency services, interested parties and community representatives would see it, owners "probably" would, and people planning to buy a flat might receive data on request (BES.media, 2025).

The social-science question

The city has assessed which apartment buildings would likely fail in a strong earthquake, but the people living in them cannot easily see those results and have little say in when, or whether, their homes will be strengthened. Who carries Almaty's earthquake risk, and who decides how it is reduced, using information that residents cannot see?

Field note 02

Background & data

Four kinds of institutions measure Almaty's building risk, and each sees a different part of the problem. The Bureau of National Statistics counts housing in "emergency condition" (аварийное). In 2025, the number of such residential buildings across Kazakhstan rose by 7% to 2.6 thousand (Kapital.kz, 2026a, citing Ranking.kz's analysis of Bureau data). But this measures physical decay, not earthquake resistance, so national statistics say nothing about seismic vulnerability. The city akimat and KazNIISA, the national construction research institute, run the seismic audits: 21,441 buildings checked in 2017–2018 and 2023–2024, with 26% failing (Kazinform, 2024). Their building-by-building results are not public (BES.media, 2025). The city's renovation programme counts "dilapidated" low-rise housing, a separate category from "not earthquake-resistant." Finally, the World Bank and researchers build risk models. The World Bank's regional model estimates that an Almaty earthquake of magnitude 6.6, a loss expected roughly once a century, would cause about US$1.4 billion in losses and 367 deaths. Its authors warn, though, that it is detailed enough only to start a policy dialogue, not to plan specific interventions (Salgado-Gálvez et al., 2024).

The chart puts the figures each source gives for the same quantity side by side. Each dot shows how far a figure sits above the lowest figure for that quantity, on one shared scale. The gaps differ a lot in size. Counts of unsafe apartment buildings almost agree: 2,339 in official statements (BES.media, 2026) against 2,364 when the audit's own tables are added up (Shokbarov & Temiraliuly, 2021), a gap of about 1%. The size of the 2017–2018 audit varies much more: 10,196 objects if the two stages in the audit paper are summed, 10,525 in the same paper's abstract, "more than 10,000" in one news report (Inbusiness, 2026), and "about 12,000" in another (BES.media, 2026). That's a spread of about 18%. Dilapidated houses fall in between: 1,366 according to the former akim in April 2025, about 1,500 in another report, and 1,403 planned for demolition by 2030 (24.kz, 2026; Kapital.kz, 2026b). Some of this spread may come from different dates and definitions. The point is not that the numbers are badly wrong. It's that there is no single authoritative number, even for the city's headline figures.

Can the numbers be trusted?

The audit itself raises harder questions. Its two stages covered different buildings: 7,027 objects in 2017 and 3,169 in 2018, with different mixes of housing and public buildings (Shokbarov & Temiraliuly, 2021). The results are far apart. In 2017, 58.9% of surveyed schools were earthquake-resistant; in 2018, 83.4% were. For apartment buildings, the figures were about 64% and 98.8%. The paper does not explain how buildings were chosen for each stage, or why the results differ so much. Its own arithmetic also slips: 4,147 resistant buildings out of 6,490 is 63.9%, not the 62.5% printed.

How the audit counts matters too. In 2017, only 39.8% of hospitals were earthquake-resistant by number of buildings, but 77.6% by floor area (Shokbarov & Temiraliuly, 2021). The larger hospitals are mostly safe and many small ones are not. So the same audit could support "most hospitals are unsafe" or "most hospital space is safe," depending on who chooses the measure.

Independence is a further question. In a 2024 discussion at the Almaty Union of Architects, recorded by journalist Askhat Niyazov, architects argued that the state is effectively absent when new buildings are accepted. That process is left to four interested parties: the client, the developer, the authorial supervision and the technical supervision (Niyazov, 2024, 31:23–31:52). They noted that Almaty has no building codes of its own, unlike Astana, and called for a full audit of buildings constructed after 2016, when oversight weakened (Niyazov, 2024, 32:04–33:11). They also pointed out that Almaty Genplan, a major design organisation, was founded by the city's Department of Urban Planning, so many assessors depend on the authority whose decisions they assess (Niyazov, 2024, 1:44:33–1:45:17).

Where the data goes silent

Four silences matter most for this project. How many people live in the flagged buildings is not published anywhere I could find. Who they are is unknown: there is no data on income, age, or whether residents are owners or renters. Buildings constructed after 2016, which the architects describe as poorly checked, are not reported as a separate group. And private houses, including homes in the foothills, sit outside the apartment-building counts entirely. The question this project asks, who carries Almaty's earthquake risk, is the question the data is least able to answer.

Field note 03

Current state of research

Research on Almaty's earthquake risk falls into four approaches. Each answers a different question, and they rarely draw on each other.

Hazard models: where could the ground shake, and how hard?

The first approach models the earthquake itself. Using high-resolution satellite elevation data, Amey et al. (2021) identified buried faults beneath and north of Almaty that had not been properly mapped. In their most damaging scenario, a magnitude 6.5 earthquake on a hidden thrust fault under the city, they estimate about 12,300 ± 5,000 completely damaged buildings and 4,100 ± 3,500 deaths. The World Bank's regional model takes a wider view. Salgado-Gálvez et al. (2024) estimate earthquake losses for five Central Asian countries at the country and oblast level. For an Almaty scenario of magnitude 6.6, they estimate about US$1.4 billion in losses and 367 deaths. The two studies model different earthquakes in different places, so their results are not directly comparable. Still, the order-of-magnitude difference in expected deaths shows how much the picture depends on the scenario chosen. Both work at the scale of the city or region: they show how dangerous a place is, not which building is dangerous.

Engineering vulnerability: which buildings would fail?

The second approach moves to individual buildings. The city's 2017–2018 certification programme, described by Shokbarov and Temiraliuly (2021), combined visual inspection of buildings, classification on the MSK-64(K) intensity scale, and electronic "passports" linked to a city map. It is the closest thing Almaty has to a building-by-building inventory. Rashid et al. (2023) used construction data from the national construction institute, KazNIISA, to assess Soviet-era residential buildings on the European Macroseismic Scale (EMS-98). They classify building types by their likely damage in strong earthquakes, with unreinforced masonry among the most vulnerable. This approach is precise about structures, but it treats a building as concrete and steel. It does not ask who lives inside, or who is told the result.

Social vulnerability: who suffers when buildings fail?

The third approach shifts the focus to people. Cutter, Boruff and Shirley (2003) built a Social Vulnerability Index for US counties, reducing 42 social and economic variables to 11 factors, such as wealth, age and housing type. They showed that the same hazard harms places differently depending on who lives there. Wisner et al. (2004) go further and argue that disasters are not purely natural. They happen where a hazard meets vulnerability produced by poverty, weak institutions and unequal power. From this angle, the key question for Almaty is not only which buildings would collapse, but why particular people still live in them, and who decides when that changes. I found no study that applies this approach to Almaty's building audits.

AI screening: can machines do the audit faster?

The fourth approach asks whether artificial intelligence can replace slow manual surveys. Aravena Pelizari et al. (2021) trained deep-learning models on Google Street View images of buildings in Santiago, Chile. Their models identified structural type, construction material and height, with agreement scores above κ = 0.81 on every task. Yu et al. (2020) built a model that flags "soft-story" buildings, a type prone to collapse because of a weak ground floor, from street-view images in California, reaching about 83–88% accuracy. Kumar et al. (2026) complicate this picture. Testing ten building attributes in a district of Lisbon, their best models reached about 81% accuracy overall, but only about 57% when every class is weighted equally. The models handled common building types well and often misclassified rare ones. For ground-floor height, a key visual cue for soft-story buildings, the best model reached only about 26%. Each of these studies also trained and tested its model within the same city. None tested whether a model built in one city works in another.

The gap

Research on Almaty tells us where the ground could shake (hazard models) and which buildings are likely to fail (engineering studies). Social-vulnerability research shows that who lives in those buildings matters as much as how they are built, but no study I found links Almaty's building audits to the people inside them. AI studies report how accurate a model is, on average, in the city where it was trained. They do not ask whether it works in a new city, whether it misses rare but dangerous buildings, or who controls and sees its results. This project sits in that gap: before asking whether AI can screen Almaty's buildings, it asks who the screening would serve.

Field note 04

The AI-assisted solution, and what it would cost

The proposal: screen the buildings nobody has checked

Almaty does not mainly need AI to find unsafe buildings it already knows about. Its audits have flagged about 5,600 (Kazinform, 2024). What it lacks is coverage of buildings outside those audits, and a fair, visible way to decide who is helped first. So the proposal is narrow.

What the AI doesA vehicle-mounted camera system collects street-level images across the city. A deep-learning model, trained on photos that local engineers have labelled, flags buildings with visible warning signs, such as a soft, open ground floor, unreinforced masonry, or added storeys. The model does not declare any building safe or unsafe. It ranks buildings for inspection, and only an engineer's visit produces a verdict.
Where it is aimedIt focuses on what the audits miss: buildings constructed after 2016, which the architects describe as poorly checked (Niyazov, 2024), and private houses outside the apartment-building counts.
Who runs itAn independent team, such as a university research group, works under a public council that includes residents. The akimat funds it but does not grade its own buildings. This responds to the architects' warning that assessors depend on the city they assess (Niyazov, 2024).
Who sees the resultsEvery resident can look up the status of their own building, and the criteria for ranking buildings are published. The city's planned digital register (Milayarova, 2026) would be open to residents by default, not only on request (BES.media, 2025).

This design is not speculative. Each part is already running somewhere.

Case study 1 · Bogotá, Colombia

Screening before an earthquake

What was built. With support from the World Bank's Global Program for Resilient Housing, Bogotá maps neighbourhoods with a vehicle-mounted mobile-mapping system and drone imagery. Open-source machine-learning algorithms flag buildings that may be vulnerable to earthquakes, such as soft-story buildings, and an engineer then inspects each one (World Bank, 2021).

What it achieved, honestly. Now in its third phase, the project covers up to 12 priority areas of about 20 square kilometres. It has produced a housing inventory and maps of where intervention may be needed, but no figures on completed inspections or retrofits (World Bank, 2021). The method is real; its effect on safety is not yet measured.

Case study 2 · Turkey, 2023

Damage mapping after an earthquake

What was built. After the February 2023 earthquakes, Microsoft's AI for Good Lab worked with Turkey's disaster agency, AFAD, to estimate building-level damage in four cities from satellite images taken in the first three days. It estimated 3,849 damaged or destroyed buildings, with 7.44% of buildings in Kahramanmaraş visibly affected (Robinson et al., 2023).

What an independent test found. Comparing Microsoft's model with Google's for health facilities, Ramachandran et al. (2025) found only fair agreement between them (κ = 0.32). Both caught just 42.9% of truly damaged facilities, often missing buildings reduced to rubble. The authors conclude that such models cannot yet replace ground inspections.

The lesson from both cases is the same: AI helps decide where humans should look first. It does not replace them. Research agrees: models miss rare building types (Kumar et al., 2026), and a photo cannot show what the architects most want checked, the foundations, rebar and concrete inside the walls (Niyazov, 2024). Environmental: small, but coal-powered. These figures are my own rough estimate, so here is how I reached them. The city plans to screen about 200,000 buildings (Milayarova, 2026). If the camera vehicle captures each building from about five angles, which helps models classify more accurately (Kumar et al., 2026), that comes to roughly one million images. A standard image-classification model on a single graphics card (GPU) can classify at least 50 images per second, so one million images would take about 20,000 seconds, under six hours. At about 300 watts, that uses under 2 kWh. Training costs more. These models are usually adapted from ones already trained on general photos (Yu et al., 2020), but allowing for several test runs, I assume about 100 GPU-hours, or roughly 30 kWh. Doubling everything for cooling, storage and other computers still gives under 100 kWh. That fits a wider pattern: classification tasks use far less energy than generative AI such as chatbots (Luccioni et al., 2024). But Kazakhstan's electricity was 62% coal in 2025 (Ember, 2026), and the larger footprint is probably the mapping vehicle driving every street, plus the drones and hardware, which I could not estimate.

Economic: cheap to flag, expensive to fix. The main costs are image collection, engineers' time labelling photos, inspections and software. The AI pays for none of the strengthening: the city's plan covers at least 200 apartment buildings by 2030 (Milayarova, 2026). Who profits: the technology vendor, and potentially developers, since a risk map can help justify demolition under the renovation programme. Who could lose: owners of flagged flats, whose property may become harder to sell. That is a plausible risk; I found no Almaty data measuring it.

Labour: the hidden human work grows. AI systems rely on human "trainers" who label data and "verifiers" who check outputs (Tubaro et al., 2020). Here that work is skilled and expensive: engineers must label the training images and inspect every flagged building. The work does not disappear. It shifts from walking every street to reviewing what the model flags, and that review must be staffed and paid.

Political and governance: the heaviest line. Kazakhstan's AI Law (No. 230-VIII), in force since 18 January 2026, classifies AI systems by risk and bans social scoring. But the system's owner assigns its risk category itself (EY, 2025), so a city-run model would grade its own risk level. Buribayeva et al. (2026) warn of a "neutrality effect" in Kazakhstan's public sector: decisions routed through technical systems can look more objective and harder to challenge even when they reproduce inequalities. They cite a closely related case, the removal of about 90,000 records from housing waiting lists using criteria that were never publicly explained. A ranking of whose building is inspected first risks the same, especially if it is trained on Almaty's inconsistent audit numbers (Section 2). The global frameworks point to the safeguards. UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) calls for human oversight and determination, which is why engineers, not the model, make the final call. The OECD AI Principles (2019) say people affected by an AI system should be able to understand and challenge its outcome, which is why residents must see their building's ranking. Finally, personal data must be stored in Kazakhstan (Law No. 94-V, art. 12), which matters if a foreign vendor supplies the system.

Data ethics: the dilemma at the centre. Street imagery captures faces, car plates and the outside of people's homes, and no one can consent on a public street. Faces and plates must be blurred and raw images deleted after use. The harder problem is disclosure. If results are public, residents of flagged buildings may face panic, falling prices or pressure to leave. If they are closed, the information gap this project began with continues, now with a more powerful tool on one side.

Verdict

Should Kazakhstan do this?

Yes, only under these conditions, and not where the city might expect.

AI screening is cheap, quick and real, but every item on the bill points to the same weakness: not the camera, but who controls and sees the result. Kazakhstan should do this only if:

  1. The AI ranks; engineers decide. No building is labelled safe or unsafe without an inspection.
  2. It targets the gaps. Post-2016 buildings and private houses come first, not buildings already audited.
  3. It is independently run and evaluated. An outside team, a published method, and a public test of how well it works in Almaty.
  4. Residents see their own results. Ranking criteria are published, and owners can look up their building, with support such as retrofit options or legal advice, not just a warning.
  5. Privacy is protected by design. Blurring, deletion of raw images, and storage in Kazakhstan.

If these conditions cannot be met, Almaty would be buying a faster way to make decisions residents still cannot see, and the honest answer is no.

The ground will move either way. The question is whether the people living on it are told.
